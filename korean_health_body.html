🇰🇷 Lesson 20: Health & Body

Nobody plans to get sick abroad, but being able to say "my throat hurts" and understand "take this three times a day" turns a scary moment into a manageable one. The timing of this lesson is deliberate. In Lesson 19 you learned must (-아야 해요), don't (-지 마세요), may I? (-아도 돼요?) and polite requests (-(으)세요). A doctor's advice is made almost entirely of those patterns, so this is where they get their real workout.

🤒 Describing Symptoms

Formula: body part + 이/가 아파요

아프다 (to hurt / be sick) is a descriptive verb, so the part that hurts is the subject and takes 이/가 (Lesson 5), not 을/를. Think "my head is hurting", not "I hurt my head".

받침 → 이: 목이 아파요, 눈이 아파요 · vowel → 가: 머리가 아파요, 허리가 아파요

💡 Why 아파요 and not 아프어요?

아프다 belongs to the ㅡ-irregular group: the ㅡ drops before 아/어, giving 아파요 (present) and 아팠어요 (past). 어지러워요 is also irregular (어지럽다, ㅂ-irregular). You'll learn the whole family in the very next lesson, Lesson 21. Today, use them as chunks. 토할 것 같아요 is a fixed phrase to learn whole.

In Korean you eat medicine: 약을 먹다. Pharmacists say it with the honorific 드시다, hence 드세요.

✅ Korean Pharmacies

Look for a sign with the big character 약 (medicine). Pharmacists are knowledgeable and many common medicines are sold over the counter. After a clinic visit you take your 처방전 (prescription) to a pharmacy, usually one right downstairs or next door. Convenience stores also sell basic painkillers and cold medicine.

빨리 나으세요 (get well soon) comes from 낫다 (to recover), an ㅅ-irregular verb: the ㅅ disappears before a vowel. Lesson 21 covers it. It's the phrase to say to any sick friend.

💡 Culture Note: Koreans take seasonal wellness seriously. When you're sick you'll be told to drink warm water, eat 죽 (rice porridge) and avoid cold drinks, and a coworker may press a packet of cold medicine or a bottle of 쌍화탕 (a herbal tonic) into your hand. Going to a clinic for a simple cold is normal and quick. The National Health Insurance makes visits cheap, and foreign residents staying longer than six months are generally enrolled too. Masks are also common and polite to wear when you have a cough.

🚨 Emergency Numbers

  • 119: ambulance and fire. Free, 24 hours, and interpretation is available.
  • 112: police.
  • 1330: Korea Travel Hotline, 24 hours, in English and other languages. They can help you find a hospital or interpret by phone.

Say: 구급차 불러 주세요! (Please call an ambulance!) and 도와주세요! (Help!)

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • 머리를 아파요 ❌ → 머리가 아파요 ✅. 아프다 is descriptive, so the body part is the subject.
  • 아프어요 ❌ → 아파요 ✅. The ㅡ drops (Lesson 21).
  • 감기가 걸렸어요 ❌ → 감기에 걸렸어요 ✅. You get "caught in" a cold: 에.
  • 약을 마셔요 for pills ❌ → 약을 먹어요 ✅.
  • 제 머리가 아파요 isn't wrong, but Koreans drop 제 when it's obvious whose head it is.
  • 술을 안 마시세요 ❌ as advice → 술을 마시지 마세요 ✅. A negative command needs -지 마세요, not 안.

Each of these mistakes is a sign your brain is generalising a rule, which is exactly what it should do. Now you're just fine-tuning which rule applies.

❓ Common Questions

What's the difference between 병원 and 의원?

Strictly, 의원 is a small clinic and 병원 is a larger hospital, and you'll see both on signs. In everyday speech Koreans call any doctor's office 병원: 병원에 가야 해요 can simply mean "I need to see a doctor".

Why does the doctor say 드세요 but I say 먹어요?

드시다 is the honorific of 먹다/마시다 (Lesson 13). Staff use it about you, the patient, to show respect. You never use honorifics about yourself, so you say 약을 먹었어요.

Is 아파요 only for pain?

No. 아프다 also means "to be sick" in general: 저 아파요 = I'm sick, 어제 아팠어요 = I was sick yesterday. Context tells you which.

How do I say I'm allergic to something specific?

Use noun + 알레르기가 있어요: 땅콩 알레르기가 있어요 (I have a peanut allergy). This is worth writing on a card to show at restaurants and pharmacies.
