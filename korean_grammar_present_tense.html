🇰🇷 Lesson 7: Verbs & the Polite Present

Until now, your Korean sentences have mostly said what things are: 저는 학생이에요, 세 시예요. Today you learn to say what people do: eat, drink, go, study, like. This is the single most useful pattern in beginner Korean. The polite present ending -아요/-어요 is what you'll hear in shops, cafés, classrooms and K-dramas all day long.

Here's the good news: Korean verbs don't change for person or number. "I eat", "you eat", "she eats", "they eat" are all 먹어요. Once you can build one form, you can talk about anyone.

🧠 The Mental Model: Dictionary Form → Stem → Ending

Every Korean verb in a dictionary ends in -다. That 다 is just a label meaning "this is a verb", a bit like the "to" in "to eat". To use the verb, you:

  1. Take the dictionary form: 먹다 (to eat)
  2. Remove 다 to find the stem: 먹
  3. Add an ending: 먹 + 어요 → 먹어요 (eat / eats)
Dictionary formStemMeaning
가다가to go
먹다먹to eat
마시다마시to drink
공부하다공부하to study

Think of the stem as the engine and the ending as the gear. Later lessons swap in new gears (past tense, "want to", "can"), but the engine stays the same. So the skill you practise today, finding the stem and attaching an ending, is one you'll use in every lesson from here on.

🎨 Vowel Harmony: 아요, 어요 or 해요?

Which ending you add depends on the last vowel of the stem. Korean likes "bright" vowels (ㅏ, ㅗ) to pair with bright vowels, and everything else to pair with the "dark" vowel ㅓ. This is called vowel harmony.

💡 Formula

1. Stem's last vowel is ㅏ or ㅗ → + 아요 · 앉다 → 앉아요, 좋다 → 좋아요

2. Any other vowel (ㅓ, ㅜ, ㅡ, ㅣ, ㅐ…) → + 어요 · 먹다 → 먹어요, 읽다 → 읽어요

3. Any verb ending in 하다 → 해요 · 공부하다 → 공부해요, 일하다 → 일해요

Only the last syllable of the stem counts: in 기다리다 the stem is 기다리, the last vowel is ㅣ, so it takes 어요.

Stems ending in a consonant: just attach

When the stem ends in a 받침, nothing merges. You simply add 아요 or 어요, and the consonant slides over into the next syllable when you speak (Lesson 3's linking rule).

하다 verbs: the easy family

하다 ("to do") becomes 해요, and so does every verb built on it. Korean makes hundreds of verbs by adding 하다 to a noun: 공부 (study) + 하다, 운동 (exercise) + 하다, 요리 (cooking) + 하다. That means one rule unlocks a huge chunk of the vocabulary.

🔀 Contractions: When Vowels Meet

When the stem ends in a vowel, adding 아요/어요 creates two vowels in a row, and Korean squeezes them together, just as English turns "do not" into "don't". These contractions aren't optional extras; they're the normal spoken and written forms. There are only a handful of patterns to learn.

💡 The Only Rule You Need to Remember

Build the long form first (보 + 아요), then say it fast. 보아요 said quickly is 봐요. 마시어요 said quickly is 마셔요. The contractions are what your mouth does naturally, so if you're stuck, slow down and rebuild.

One more pattern, as a preview: stems ending in ㅡ drop it (바쁘다 → 바빠요 "be busy", 쓰다 → 써요 "write"). You'll study this family properly in Lesson 21, along with the true irregulars.

📦 The Object Marker 을 / 를

In Lesson 5 you met the topic marker (은/는) and the subject marker (이/가). The third big label is the object marker, which tags the thing that receives the action: the coffee you drink, the book you read. Because Korean puts the verb last, the object comes before the verb: "I coffee drink".

💡 Formula

Noun with 받침 + 을 → 밥을, 책을, 물을

Noun without 받침 + 를 → 커피를, 영화를, 한국어를

Pattern: (Topic 은/는) + Object 을/를 + Verb-아요/어요

KoreanRomanizationEnglish

Notice that one form, 먹어요, covers "I eat" (habit), "I'm eating" (right now) and even "I'll eat" (very near future, from context). Korean's present tense is broad, so let context do the work.

⚠️ 좋아하다 vs 좋다: a subtle but important pair

좋아하다 is an action verb, "to like", and takes an object: 저는 커피를 좋아해요 (I like coffee). 좋다 is a descriptive verb, "to be good", and takes a subject: 커피가 좋아요 (Coffee is good / I like coffee). Both are natural; just keep each with its own particle.

🌈 Adjectives Are Verbs Too

This surprises almost every English speaker: in Korean, adjectives like "expensive", "tired" or "delicious" are descriptive verbs. They have dictionary forms ending in 다 and conjugate with exactly the same rules. The "is" is already built in.

Spot the grammar: 마셔요 (ㅣ + 어 → ㅕ), 배워요 (ㅜ + 어 → ㅝ), 공부해요 (하다), 한국어를 and 커피를 (object, no 받침), 숙제가 많아요 (descriptive verb with a subject), and 사요 used for "I'll buy" right now. Also notice 뭐 마셔요? drops the object marker: in casual questions 뭐를 usually shrinks to just 뭐.

🌏 Cultural Note — 밥 먹었어요? 밥 means "cooked rice", but it also means "a meal" in general, because rice is at the heart of Korean eating. That's why 밥을 먹어요 simply means "I eat (a meal)". Koreans even greet friends with "Have you eaten?" (밥 먹었어요?), a warm check-in rather than a literal question. You'll learn that past-tense ending in Lesson 15, but you can already recognise 밥 and 먹 inside it.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Adding 이에요 to an adjective. "It's expensive" is 비싸요, never 비싸예요. The "is" is already inside descriptive verbs; 이에요 is only for nouns.
  • Looking at the wrong vowel. Only the last syllable of the stem decides: 만나다 → 만나요 (ㅏ), 배우다 → 배워요 (ㅜ).
  • Forgetting to contract. 가아요 and 마시어요 aren't used; say 가요 and 마셔요. (보아요 does exist in careful writing, but 봐요 is the everyday form.)
  • Using 을/를 with a descriptive verb. 커피를 맛있어요 is wrong; say 커피가 맛있어요.
  • Mixing up 좋다 and 좋아하다. 커피를 좋아요 is wrong; say 커피를 좋아해요 or 커피가 좋아요.

If a verb ever refuses to follow these rules (듣다 → 들어요, 덥다 → 더워요), it isn't you making a mistake; it's one of Korean's irregular verbs, and they get their own lesson later. For now, every verb in this lesson plays fair.

❓ Common Questions

Is 먹어요 "I eat", "I'm eating" or "I will eat"?

All three, depending on context. The polite present covers habits, actions happening right now, and plans for the very near future. Korean does have a dedicated progressive (-고 있어요) and future (-(으)ㄹ 거예요), which you'll meet in Lessons 25 and 17, but the simple present is what people use most of the time.

How do I ask a question with these verbs?

Just raise your intonation at the end: 커피를 마셔요. (I drink coffee.) → 커피를 마셔요? (Do you drink coffee?) The written form is identical except for the question mark. Question words work like in Lesson 5: 뭐 먹어요? (What are you eating?)

Can I say 공부를 해요 instead of 공부해요?

Yes. Many 하다 verbs can be split, treating the noun as an object: 공부를 해요, 운동을 해요. The meaning is the same. The split form is handy when you want to add something, e.g. 한국어 공부를 해요 (I do Korean study). You'll see how this affects negation in Lesson 10.

Is this polite enough for everyone?

The -아요/-어요 ending (해요체) is polite and safe with strangers, shopkeepers, colleagues and older people in everyday situations. Very formal settings, like news broadcasts or presentations, use -습니다, which you'll practise in Lesson 13.
