🇰🇷 Lesson 6: Numbers, Time & Dates

Here is the one surprise this lesson has for you: Korean has two complete sets of numbers, and everyday life uses both. That sounds like double the work, but English does something similar. You say "one, two, three" and also "uni-, bi-, tri-" (unicycle, bicycle, tricycle). Korean's second set is simply used much more often. Once you know which set goes with which job, it stops being confusing and becomes a pattern you can rely on.

💡 The Mental Model: Two Toolboxes

Sino-Korean (한자어 수, from Chinese) is the measuring toolbox: dates, money, minutes, phone numbers, floors, big numbers.

Native Korean (고유어 수) is the counting toolbox: counting things and people, hours, age.

A good first guess: if you are counting how many, reach for Native. If you are reading a label or a measurement, reach for Sino.

Used for: dates, money, phone numbers, addresses, minutes, floors, and every number above 99.

Sino numbers are built like Lego: how many tens + ten + ones. So 35 is 삼십오 (three-ten-five), 247 is 이백사십칠, and this year, 2026, is 이천이십육. Two rules keep you accurate:

✅ Two Building Rules

1. No "one" in front of 십, 백, 천, 만. 10 is 십 (not 일십), 100 is 백, 10,000 is 만. (억 is the exception: 100 million is 일억.)

2. Korean groups big numbers by FOUR digits, not three. English thinks in thousands; Korean thinks in 만 (ten-thousands). 50,000 is 오만 ("five ten-thousands"), 150,000 is 십오만. This matters every time you pay for something.

🔢 Native Korean Numbers (고유어 수)

Used for: counting with counters, hours on the clock, and age. Native numbers only go up to 99; above that, Koreans switch to Sino.

Teens are 열 + ones: 11 = 열하나, 15 = 열다섯. Twenties are 스물 + ones: 23 = 스물셋.

⚠️ Five Numbers Shorten Before a Counter

하나 → 한 · 둘 → 두 · 셋 → 세 · 넷 → 네 · 스물 → 스무

This also happens inside bigger numbers: 열한 개 (11 items), 스물두 살 (22 years old). Think of it like English "a" becoming "an" — a small sound change, not a new word.

📏 Counters (단위) — This Lesson's Grammar

In English you can say "three books" directly. Korean always adds a counter (a unit word), the way English says "three cups of coffee" or "two sheets of paper" — except Korean does it for everything.

💡 The Formula

Noun + Number + Counter

커피 두 잔 = coffee + two + cups → "two cups of coffee"
학생 세 명 = student + three + people → "three students"

Notice the order: the noun comes first, and the number and counter follow it.

📅 Days, Months & Dates

Days of the week all end in 요일. Months are just Sino number + 월, and dates are Sino number + 일.

📞 Phone numbers and age

Phone numbers are read digit by digit in Sino, usually with 공 for zero. The hyphen is often spoken as 에: 010-1234-5678 → 공일공에 일이삼사에 오육칠팔. Age in everyday speech uses Native + 살 (스물다섯 살); forms and formal documents use Sino + 세 (이십오 세).

🧩 Grammar in Action: Numbers + 이에요/예요

From Lesson 5 you know Noun + 이에요/예요 ("it is …"): 이에요 after a final consonant (받침), 예요 after a vowel. Numbers with counters behave exactly like nouns, so the same rule applies — just look at the last syllable:

To ask, use 몇 ("how many / what number") + counter, then 이에요/예요 with a rising voice: 몇 시예요? (What time is it?), 몇 살이에요? (How old are you?), 몇 명이에요? (How many people?), 몇 월이에요? (What month is it?). For days of the week, ask 무슨 요일이에요? For the date, ask 며칠이에요? — spelled 며칠, never "몇 일". The full set of question words arrives in Lesson 10; for now, these are handy fixed patterns.

💡 이/가 with New Information

Lesson 5's subject marker shows up naturally here: 생일이 언제예요? (When is your birthday?) The asker doesn't know the answer, so the thing being asked about takes 이/가. In the answer, the birthday becomes the topic: 제 생일은 오월 오 일이에요.

🌏 Culture Note — Age and the Number 4: Traditionally Koreans counted everyone as one year old at birth and added a year every New Year's Day ("Korean age"). Since June 2023 the law uses international age (만 나이) for official purposes, but you will still hear older counting in conversation, so don't be surprised if someone's "age" is one or two years more than you expect. Asking age early is normal, because it decides how people speak to each other (Lesson 13). And watch the elevator: 사 (four) sounds like the character for death, so many buildings label the 4th floor F instead of 4.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • 삼 시 for 3 o'clock → hours are Native: 세 시.
  • 세 시 세 분 for 3:03 → minutes are Sino: 세 시 삼 분.
  • 셋 개 → shorten before a counter: 세 개. Likewise 스무 살, not 스물 살.
  • 일만 원 in speech → just 만 원.
  • 몇 일 → the correct spelling is 며칠.
  • 육월, 십월 → 유월, 시월.
  • 세 개 사과 (English order) → noun first: 사과 세 개.

If you mix up the two systems a few times this week, good — that means you are actually using them. Every Korean learner says 삼 시 at least once. Mistakes here are just feedback about which toolbox to reach for.

❓ Common Questions

Why does Korean need two number systems?

Native numbers are the original Korean words. Sino-Korean numbers came in with Chinese vocabulary over many centuries and took over "official" jobs like dates, money and mathematics. Both survived, each keeping its own territory — much like English keeps both "hearty" (native) and "cordial" (borrowed).

What do Koreans do for Native numbers above 99?

They switch to Sino-Korean. You would count a hundred apples as 사과 백 개. In practice many people also switch to Sino for large counts well below 100, especially in fast speech, so don't worry if you hear 사과 삼십 개.

영 or 공 for zero?

공 is the everyday choice when reading digits one by one (phone numbers, room numbers, bus numbers). 영 is used in maths, temperatures and scores.

Do I really need to learn every counter?

Not yet. 개 (things), 명 (people), 잔 (drinks), 살 (age) and 시 (hours) carry you through most beginner situations, and 개 is often accepted in casual speech when you forget the specific one. Add the others as you meet them.

🌟 Going Further

  • Read your own phone number and birthday aloud in Korean, then say your age both ways (Native + 살 and Sino + 세).
  • Describe one weekday in times: 일곱 시예요 when you wake up, the time of lunch, the time you finish. Write each as a full 이에요/예요 sentence.
