🇰🇷 Lesson 1: Hangul Consonants

🎯 What You'll Learn

The Korean alphabet (한글, Hangul) is one of the most scientific writing systems ever created. In this lesson you'll master all 19 consonants — 14 basic and 5 double (tense) consonants.

Estimated Time: 45–60 minutes

📖 The Story of Hangul

Hangul was created in 1443 by King Sejong the Great so that ordinary people could read and write. Before Hangul, Korea used Chinese characters (한자), which required years of study. Sejong designed Hangul to be so logical that "a wise person can learn it in one morning, and even a fool can learn it in ten days."

💡 Fun Fact: Hangul's consonant shapes are based on the shape of the mouth, tongue, and throat when making each sound. It's literally a diagram of how you speak!

🔤 The 14 Basic Consonants

Korean has 14 basic consonant letters. Each has a name and represents a specific sound. Pay attention to how the sound can change depending on its position in a syllable.

✅ Pattern to Notice

Plain → Aspirated: Some consonants come in pairs. The aspirated version releases a stronger puff of air (hold your hand in front of your mouth — you'll feel the difference):

ㄱ (g) → ㅋ (k)  ·  ㄷ (d) → ㅌ (t)  ·  ㅂ (b) → ㅍ (p)  ·  ㅈ (j) → ㅊ (ch)

💪 The 5 Double (Tense) Consonants

Double consonants are written by repeating the basic consonant. They're pronounced with a tight, tense throat — no puff of air, but with extra force. English doesn't really have this sound, so it takes practice!

⚠️ The Three-Way Distinction

Korean has three types of consonants for each group — this is the trickiest part for English speakers:

Plain (lenis): ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ  ·  /k t p tɕ/

Aspirated: ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ  ·  /kʰ tʰ pʰ tɕʰ/

Tense (fortis, doubled): ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅉ ㅆ  ·  /k͈ t͈ p͈ tɕ͈/

Practice by holding a tissue in front of your mouth: the tissue should flutter for aspirated, not for tense, and barely for plain.

🔬 Why "a puff of air" isn't the whole story

All three series are voiceless at the start of a word — none of them is the English "b" or "g". What separates them is voice onset time: how long the vocal folds wait before they start buzzing.

SeriesExampleTypical VOTWhat your throat does
Tense까~12 msGlottis tight, voicing almost immediate
Plain (lenis)가~30–50 msRelaxed — and yes, slightly aspirated
Aspirated카~80–100+ msA long breathy delay before the vowel

So the plain series is not unaspirated — it simply aspirates less. And in Seoul Korean the contrast is currently shifting: among younger speakers plain and aspirated VOT have converged, and the cue that now separates them is the pitch of the following vowel (aspirated → high, plain → low). If you can hear the difference in pitch but not in breath, you are hearing modern Korean correctly.

🧠 How Consonant Shapes Were Designed

The Hunminjeongeum Haerye (훈민정음 해례본, 1446) — the commentary that explains the alphabet — describes three separate mechanisms. Most introductions blur them together, which makes Hangul look more arbitrary than it is. Kept apart, the whole system falls out of five drawings and one rule.

1. Five pictographs (象形)

Exactly five letters are pictures of the vocal tract. Click each diagram to hear it.

⚠️ A myth worth unlearning

You will often read that ㅂ is the mouth seen from the side, that ㄷ is the tongue against the tooth ridge, or that ㅎ is air streaming out of the throat. None of these appear in the Haerye. All three letters are derived by adding strokes, and derived letters are given no picture of their own — they inherit meaning from their base. ㅂ is labial because ㅁ is; ㄷ is lingual because ㄴ is.

2. Stroke addition (加畫)

Every remaining consonant is built by adding a stroke to a base, on an explicit phonetic principle. The Haerye puts it plainly:

ㅋ比ㄱ，聲出稍厲，故加畫。
"Compared with ㄱ, the sound of ㅋ issues forth somewhat more fiercely (厲) — therefore a stroke is added."

A stroke is not decoration. It is a visual mark of a phonetically stronger sound.

ㄱ +획 → ㅋ ㄴ +획 → ㄷ +획 → ㅌ ㅁ +획 → ㅂ +획 → ㅍ ㅅ +획 → ㅈ +획 → ㅊ ㅇ +획 → ㆆ +획 → ㅎ

ㆆ (여린히읗, a glottal stop) is the missing rung most charts skip. It fell out of use, but without it the guttural series ㅇ → ㅎ looks like it jumps two strokes at once.

3. Doubling (各自並書)

The tense consonants are a different mechanism entirely — not the end of a stroke-addition chain. The letter is simply written twice, side by side.

ㄱ병서 → ㄲ ㄷ병서 → ㄸ ㅂ병서 → ㅃ ㅅ병서 → ㅆ ㅈ병서 → ㅉ

The 1446 document actually lists six doubled letters. The sixth, ㆅ (쌍히읗), is long obsolete.

The letters left over

Three letters belong to none of the above. The Haerye calls them 異體字 — "letters of a different form" — and says of them:

半舌音ㄹ，半齒音ㅿ … 異其體，無加畫之義焉。
"The semi-lingual ㄹ and the semi-dental ㅿ … differ in their form; there is no stroke-addition principle in them."

ㄹ, along with the obsolete ㆁ and ㅿ, sits outside the system by design. That is why you will never find ㄹ in a derivation tree — and why its absence is a fact about Hangul, not an oversight in the chart.

🎯 Summary

🎉 Key Takeaways

  • Korean has 19 consonants: 14 basic + 5 double (tense)
  • Consonant shapes are based on how your mouth makes the sound
  • Korean uses a three-way distinction: plain, aspirated, and tense
  • Consonants can sound different at the beginning vs. end of a syllable (받침)
  • The aspirated consonants (ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ) add a puff of air to the basic sounds
