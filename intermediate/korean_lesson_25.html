🇰🇷 Lesson 25: Korean in the Wild

You have spent twenty-four lessons learning careful, standard Seoul Korean. Then you step off the KTX in Busan and a shop owner asks 어데서 왔노? Your friend texts 퇴근 ㅇㅋ? 치맥 ㄱㄱ? A variety show flashes 갑분싸 across the screen in giant yellow letters. None of it looks like your textbook — and all of it is completely normal Korean.

This lesson is a field guide. The goal is mainly recognition: understanding what you hear and read in dramas, variety shows, group chats and outside Seoul. Along the way you will learn which parts are safe to use yourself, and which are better left to native speakers and close friends.

🧠 The Mental Model: Standard Korean Is One Variety Among Many

표준어 (standard Korean) is officially defined as the educated speech of modern Seoul. It's what news anchors, textbooks and TOPIK use, and it's what you should produce in formal situations. But almost nobody speaks only 표준어. Real Korean varies along three axes:

AxisKorean termWhat variesExample
Region사투리 / 방언Endings, some words, intonation어디 가? → 어디 가노? (경상도)
Generation & medium신조어, 줄임말, 유행어New coinages, abbreviations, chat shorthand얼어 죽어도 아이스 아메리카노 → 얼죽아
Borrowing외래어, 콩글리시English (and other) words, often with new meanings노트북 = laptop, not notebook

💡 The Golden Rule

Recognise widely, produce carefully. Understanding slang and dialect makes you a better listener; using them before you know their social weight can make you sound rude, childish or like you are mocking someone. When in doubt, speak 표준어 — nobody will ever be offended by it.

🗺️ 사투리: Regional Dialects

South Korea is small, but its dialects are strikingly different, and dramas love them: a character from 부산 or 전라도 is instantly recognisable. You don't need to speak any dialect, but you should recognise the big three signatures.

경상도 (Busan, Daegu, Ulsan): Tone and Question Endings

Gyeongsang speech is the dialect most foreigners notice first. It sounds "strong" or "blunt" to Seoul ears because of its pitch accent — word meaning can depend on high and low pitch, unlike standard Korean. Its most learnable feature is the question system:

충청도 and 제주

충청도 speech is stereotyped as slow and indirect, with the ending -유 in place of -요: 괜찮아유, 그래유. The indirectness is part of the joke: a 충청도 speaker might say "that's pretty good, I guess" when they mean "no".

제주 is a different story. Jeju speech (제주어) is so different from standard Korean that many linguists treat it as a separate language, and UNESCO lists it as critically endangered. Mainland Koreans often need subtitles for it. Recognise a few famous phrases:

제주어표준어English
혼저 옵서예어서 오세요Welcome (seen on signs all over Jeju)
하영많이a lot
폭싹 속았수다수고 많으셨습니다You've worked so hard — 속다 here means "to toil", not "to be fooled"; also the title of a 2025 drama

🌏 Cultural note: Dialects carry identity and pride. Many people from Busan or Gwangju switch to 표준어 at work in Seoul and back to 사투리 on the phone with their parents — a kind of code-switching you already practised with 반말 and 존댓말. Imitating a dialect to be funny can come across as mocking, especially from a foreigner. Enjoying it, asking about it and learning a phrase from a local friend, on the other hand, usually earns you a lot of goodwill.

⚠️ Slang With a Hidden Edge

Some very common slang is built on vulgar roots. The intensifier prefix 개- is crude but widespread among young people; another intensifier prefix, 존-, which you will see in online food reviews, comes from a genuinely vulgar word — never use it. The adjective 미쳤다 ("crazy") is used as praise among friends (이 노래 미쳤다!), but to an older person it just sounds like an insult. If you can't explain where a slang word comes from, don't say it to your boss.

🔤 Konglish & 외래어: Borrowed Words With New Lives

외래어 are loanwords that have become Korean. Most came from English, but some arrived via Japanese or German, and many changed meaning on the way. Words coined in Korea from English parts are nicknamed 콩글리시 (Konglish). Treat them as Korean words — English speakers abroad won't understand them.

💡 Which One? Konglish vs Native Word

Many borrowed words compete with a native or Sino-Korean word, and the choice signals register. 핸드폰 is casual; 휴대폰 or 휴대 전화 is more formal. 헬스 is everyday; 운동 is general. On official documents and in news you will see the Sino-Korean version (휴대 전화, 노트북 컴퓨터), in conversation the short loanword. The official spelling of "fighting" is 파이팅, though 화이팅 is extremely common in real life.

📺 Drama & Variety Korean

Dramas and variety shows are wonderful input, but they are not neutral samples of speech. A few things to expect:

📰 Reading: 신조어, 어떻게 봐야 할까

Korean
요즘 청소년들은 하루가 멀다 하고 새로운 줄임말을 만든다. '얼죽아'나 '갓생'처럼 금방 널리 퍼지는 말도 있지만, 몇 달 만에 사라지는 말도 많다. 부모가 자녀의 문자 메시지를 이해하지 못해 당황하는 일도 흔하다. 그래서 일부에서는 줄임말이 한국어를 망친다고 걱정한다. 그러나 언어학자들은 새로운 말이 생기고 사라지는 것은 어느 언어에서나 볼 수 있는 자연스러운 현상이라고 설명한다. 중요한 것은 상황에 맞게 말을 골라 쓰는 능력이다. 친구와의 대화에서는 신조어를 즐기되, 공식적인 자리에서는 표준어를 쓰는 것이 바람직하다.
English translation

These days teenagers create new abbreviations almost every day. Some words, like "eoljuka" (iced Americano even if I freeze) or "gatsaeng" (a productive life), spread widely right away, but many disappear within a few months. It is also common for parents to be baffled because they can't understand their children's text messages. So some people worry that abbreviations are ruining the Korean language. Linguists, however, explain that new words appearing and disappearing is a natural phenomenon seen in every language. What matters is the ability to choose your words to suit the situation. It is desirable to enjoy new words in conversation with friends, but to use standard Korean in formal settings.

Two written-style items worth noting: 하루가 멀다 하고 ("almost every day", literally "saying a day is too far") is itself an idiom, and -되 in 즐기되 is a written "but / while" that sets a condition on the first clause: "enjoy them, but…".

⚠️ Common Mistakes

⚠️ Watch Out For

Chat shorthand to seniors. ㅇㅇ or ㅇㅋ to your manager reads as curt, even rude. Write 네, 알겠습니다.

Using slang in writing tests. TOPIK and any formal writing expect 표준어. 대박, 근데 and 완전 will cost you in an essay: use 매우, 그런데/그러나, 아주.

Performing a dialect. Throwing in 뭐 하노? as a joke with someone from Busan can sound like mimicry. Answer in 표준어; let them enjoy teaching you.

Using Konglish abroad. Ask an American for a "one room" or "eye shopping" and you'll get blank looks — and 노트북 is not a notebook.

Assuming slang is timeless. Many 신조어 fade within a year; using an outdated one sounds like a parent trying to be cool. Mainstream (🟢) items are the safe investment.

❓ Common Questions

Should I learn a dialect?

For most learners, no — learn to understand the main features and speak 표준어. If you live in Busan or Gwangju, though, you'll absorb some naturally, and a few local words used with friends will make people smile. Just keep 표준어 for work and formal settings.

Is Konglish "wrong" Korean?

No. 노트북, 원룸 and 핸드폰 are real Korean words, used by everyone. They only become a problem if you assume they mean the same thing in English. Think of them as Korean vocabulary that happens to look English.

How do I keep up with new slang?

You don't have to keep up with everything — native speakers over thirty don't either. When you meet a new word, search it in a Korean portal (the explanations are often written for confused adults) and check whether it's still in use. Focus on items that appear in mainstream media.

Will slang appear on TOPIK?

TOPIK tests standard Korean, so slang rarely appears as a question target. But common loanwords and mainstream coinages can appear in reading passages, and a passage about language change (like the one above) is exactly the kind of topic TOPIK likes.

🌟 Going Further

Challenge 1: Watch ten minutes of a drama set in Busan or Jeolla-do with Korean subtitles. List five dialect endings or words and write their 표준어 equivalents.

Challenge 2: Write a TOPIK-style 한다체 paragraph (about 300 characters) answering: 줄임말과 신조어 사용에 대해 어떻게 생각하는가? Give one advantage, one disadvantage and your opinion — using no slang yourself.

If a fast variety show still leaves you lost, that's normal — it's one of the hardest listening tasks even at C1, full of overlapping voices, dialect and in-jokes. Every episode you watch trains your ear a little more, and the day you laugh at a caption before reading the subtitle is a real milestone.
