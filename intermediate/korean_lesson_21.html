🇰🇷 Lesson 21: Reading Written Korean: 한다체, News & Headlines

Open any Korean news app and the first thing you see is a headline like 물가 또 올라 or 정부, 내년 최저임금 동결하기로. No 요, no 습니다, particles missing, sentences that seem to stop halfway. Tap the article and every sentence ends in a bare -다: 올랐다, 밝혔다, 보인다. You have spent twenty lessons learning to talk to people. This lesson is about the voice Korean uses when it talks to nobody in particular — the voice of newspapers, textbooks, diaries, essays and the TOPIK writing paper.

The good news: this is mostly a recognition skill, and it is far more regular than spoken Korean. Once you know about a dozen endings and a handful of news formulas, pages that looked like a wall of text start to open up.

🧠 The Mental Model: The Voice of the Page

Every Korean speech style you know encodes a relationship: 해요체 for polite strangers, 합니다체 for formal audiences, 반말 for close friends. Written Korean needs a style for a situation with no specific listener — a reporter writing for millions, a student writing an essay, you writing in your diary. That style is the plain style, usually called 한다체 ("the 한다 style", named after how 하다 looks in it).

💡 Why It Isn't Rude

Grammatically, 한다체 is the lowest speech level (called 해라체 in grammar books). Spoken to a person's face, it sounds like a parent talking to a child or an older friend to a much younger one. But on the page there is no "you" being addressed, so there is nobody to be rude to. That is why a newspaper can write 대통령이 말했다 about the president without any disrespect — and why the same sentence said aloud to your boss would be a disaster.

You already hear this style in speech in one situation: talking to yourself. When a Korean steps outside and mutters 아, 춥다 or looks out the window and says 어, 비 온다, that is 한다체 — a remark addressed to nobody.

🧩 Building the Present Tense

The present tense is the only part with real choices, and the choice depends on one question: is it an action verb or a descriptive verb (adjective)? Note that the 한다체 present is often not the dictionary form, even though both end in -다.

✅ The Formula

Action verb, vowel stem: stem + -ㄴ다 → 가다 → 간다, 보다 → 본다, 공부하다 → 공부한다

Action verb, ㄹ stem: drop ㄹ, add -ㄴ다 → 살다 → 산다, 알다 → 안다, 만들다 → 만든다

Action verb, consonant stem: stem + -는다 → 먹다 → 먹는다, 읽다 → 읽는다, 듣다 → 듣는다

Adjective: = dictionary form → 크다, 바쁘다, 중요하다, 춥다

이다 / 아니다: 학생이다, 의사이다 or 의사다 (이 often drops after a vowel), 학생이 아니다

Notice the irregular verbs: -는다 starts with a consonant, so ㄷ- and ㅂ-irregulars stay unchanged (듣는다, 돕는다) — the same rule of thumb you learned for -고 and -지만. Only ㄹ-stems change, because ㄹ always drops before ㄴ.

⚠️ 있다 Has Two Faces

For existence and possession, 있다 behaves like an adjective: 시간이 있다, 문제가 있다. But when it means "to stay (somewhere)" it is an action, and you will see 있는다: 주말에는 집에 있는다 ("I stay home at weekends"). The same goes for 없다 (always 없다) and compounds like 재미있다 (always 재미있다).

Two things to notice. First, formal writing keeps forms "uncontracted" more often than speech: 하였다 beside 했다, 되었다 beside 됐다, 것이다 rather than spoken 거다. Both appear in newspapers; 하였다 simply feels a notch more formal. Second, written questions like -는가 are usually rhetorical — an essay or editorial asks 무엇이 문제인가? and then answers it. Nobody is expected to reply.

한다체 vs 반말: Which One?

Learners who studied 반말 in Lesson 1 often mix the two, because both drop 요. They are different levels with different jobs:

💡 The Handful of Hanja You'll Actually Meet

Korean newspapers still use a few single Chinese characters in headlines to save space, above all for countries: 韓 (한국), 美 (미국), 中 (중국), 日 (일본), 北 (북한), and 與野 (여야, the ruling and opposition parties). You don't need to learn hanja to read the news — just recognise these. Lesson 23 shows how the Sino-Korean sounds behind them help you guess thousands of words.

Also watch for the endings that turn up at the end of headlines again and again: 예정 (scheduled), 전망 (expected), 추진 (being pushed forward), 검토 (under review), 우려 (concern that…), 논란 (controversy), 비상 (emergency). 정부, 증세 검토 = "Government reviewing tax increase".

🔍 A Reading Strategy That Works

  1. Headline first. Rebuild it into a full sentence in your head — add the particles and the missing -했다.
  2. Lede (first sentence) second. Korean articles put who/what/when/where in sentence one. If you only understand one sentence, make it this one.
  3. Jump to the end of each sentence. Korean saves the main verb for last. Find it (올랐다? 밝혔다? 보인다?) before untangling the middle.
  4. Chop at the connectors. Long news sentences are chains joined by -며, -고, -아/어 and -(으)나. Split them and read one link at a time.
  5. Spot the source. 에 따르면 and -다고 밝혔다 tell you whose claim it is — the reporter's or someone else's.
  6. Guess, then check. Unknown words in news are mostly Sino-Korean compounds. Guess from parts you know before reaching for the dictionary.
🇰🇷 Culture note: Korean schoolchildren keep a 그림일기 (picture diary) and later an ordinary 일기 for homework, always in 한다체 — so for Koreans the style feels personal and everyday, not just "newspaper language". Many adults still keep diaries or write personal blogs in it. If you start a Korean diary, one to three sentences a day in 한다체 is one of the best habits you can build at this level.

📰 Reading: A Heat-Wave Report

기사
서울 첫 폭염주의보… 평년보다 열흘 빨라
기상청에 따르면 어제 오후 서울 지역에 올해 첫 폭염주의보가 내려졌다. 이는 평년보다 열흘가량 빠른 것이다. 서울의 낮 최고 기온은 34도를 기록했으며, 밤에도 기온이 25도 아래로 내려가지 않는 열대야가 나타났다. 기상청 관계자는 "이번 더위는 다음 주 중반까지 계속될 것으로 보인다"고 밝혔다. 이에 따라 서울시는 무더위 쉼터를 늘리고 노인 등 취약 계층의 건강 관리에 나설 예정이다. 한편 냉방기 사용이 크게 늘면서 정부는 시민들에게 에너지 절약을 당부했다.
English translation

Seoul's first heat-wave advisory… ten days earlier than usual. According to the Korea Meteorological Administration, Seoul was placed under its first heat-wave advisory of the year yesterday afternoon. This is about ten days earlier than in an average year. Seoul's daytime high reached 34 degrees, and there was a "tropical night", when the temperature does not fall below 25 degrees even at night. An official from the KMA said, "This heat looks likely to continue until the middle of next week." Accordingly, the city of Seoul plans to open more cooling shelters and to step up health care for vulnerable groups such as the elderly. Meanwhile, as air-conditioner use has risen sharply, the government has urged citizens to save energy.

Look at how the article uses this lesson's toolkit: the headline ends on a bare -아/어 (빨라); the lede begins with 에 따르면; the third sentence is a chain joined by -며; the official's words are wrapped in -고 밝혔다; the forecast is hedged with -(으)ㄹ 것으로 보인다; 이에 따라 introduces the city's response; 한편 switches to a side topic.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

1. Using the dictionary form for an action in the present. ✗ 나는 매일 커피를 마시다. → ✓ 나는 매일 커피를 마신다. The dictionary form is only the present tense for adjectives.

2. Giving adjectives the verb ending. ✗ 날씨가 좋는다 / 예쁜다 → ✓ 날씨가 좋다 / 예쁘다.

3. Forgetting the ㄹ drop. ✗ 알는다, 만들는다 → ✓ 안다, 만든다.

4. Mixing styles in one text. An essay that says 환경 문제가 심각하다 and then 우리가 노력해야 해요 sounds like it switched speakers. Pick 한다체 and stay in it.

5. Using 한다체 to people. Writing 내일 간다 in a message to your teacher is not "written style" — it is talking down to them. 한다체 is for texts without an addressee.

  4. 기상청은 더위가 다음 주 _____까지 이어질 것으로 본다. (Answer: 중반)
  5. 서울시는 무더위 _____를 늘릴 예정이다. (Answer: 쉼터)
  6. 냉방기 사용이 늘자 정부는 시민들에게 _____ 절약을 당부했다. (Answer: 에너지)

❓ Common Questions

Should I say 했다 or 하였다?

Both are correct. 했다 is the everyday written form; 하였다 feels more formal and is common in official documents, academic writing and many model TOPIK answers. Choose one and use it consistently within a text.

Is the dictionary form ever the present tense?

Yes — for adjectives (좋다, 크다, 필요하다), for 이다/아니다 and for existence 있다/없다. For action verbs, the dictionary form is only a label in the dictionary; the present tense needs -ㄴ다/-는다.

Do I need 한다체 for the TOPIK?

For TOPIK II writing questions 53 and 54 (the chart description and the essay), you are expected to write in 한다체 — the official model answers use it, and switching to -아요 or -습니다 style is treated as a language-use error. Questions 51 and 52 are different: you fill blanks in a given text, so you match the style that text already uses. Reading passages throughout the exam are full of 한다체 and news formulas, so this lesson pays off on both papers.

What about -느냐, -(으)냐 and -니 questions?

-니? and -냐? are the spoken 해라체 questions (a mother to a child: 밥 먹었니?). -(느)냐 also appears in the written quotations you met in reported speech (-냐고). The written, essay-style question is -는가/-(으)ㄴ가.

Why do some headlines end in -다 and others don't?

Some outlets and online news write complete 한다체 sentences as headlines, especially for quotes. The telegraphic noun or -아/어 endings are the traditional print style. Both are normal; the reading skill is the same — rebuild the full sentence in your head.

Reading the news in a foreign language is one of the classic points where a B1 learner feels they have hit a wall — it is slower and denser than anything in a textbook. That feeling is normal and temporary. Pick one short article a day, rebuild the headline, read the first two sentences carefully and stop. In a month, the formulas in this lesson will jump out at you before you have consciously looked for them.
