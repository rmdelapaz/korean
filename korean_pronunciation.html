🇰🇷 Lesson 3: Pronunciation Rules

You can now read every letter. So why does 한국어 sound like "han-gu-geo", and why is 감사합니다 said with an "m"? Korean spelling keeps each word's building blocks visible — 먹다, 먹어, 먹는 all show the same stem 먹 — while the sounds adjust smoothly where syllables meet. The good news: these adjustments are predictable. A handful of rules covers almost everything you'll hear at A1, and none of them are exceptions to memorise one by one; they are simply what a mouth does when two sounds collide.

📖 받침 (Batchim) — Final Consonants

받침 literally means "support" — it's the consonant at the bottom of a syllable block. Korean has 27 possible 받침 spellings (16 single letters and 11 double-consonant clusters), but at the end of a syllable they reduce to only 7 sounds:

Pronounced As받침 LettersExampleReading
ㄱ [k]ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ국, 부엌, 밖guk, bueok, bak
ㄴ [n]ㄴ눈, 산nun, san
ㄷ [t]ㄷ, ㅌ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅎ맛, 있다, 꽃mat, itda, kkot
ㄹ [l]ㄹ달, 물dal, mul
ㅁ [m]ㅁ밤, 김bam, gim
ㅂ [p]ㅂ, ㅍ밥, 앞bap, ap
ㅇ [ng]ㅇ강, 방gang, bang

⚠️ Key Insight

Seven 받침 letters (ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ, ㄷ) ALL sound like [t] when at the end of a syllable. This is why 맛 (taste) and 맞 (correct) sound the same in isolation!

Why does this happen? A Korean final stop is unreleased: your tongue or lips close and stay closed, with no puff of air afterward. Without a release, the differences between ㅅ, ㅈ, ㅊ and ㅌ simply have nowhere to be heard — they all end as the same silent closure at the tooth ridge.

Double 받침 (겹받침)

Eleven clusters such as ㄳ, ㄺ and ㅄ write two consonants at the bottom. At the end of a word, or before another consonant, only one of them is pronounced. Usually it's the left one; ㄺ and ㄻ normally keep the right one. (There are a few exceptions, such as 밟다 [밥따] — you'll pick them up word by word.)

WordClusterPronouncedMeaning
값ㅄ → ㅂ[갑]price
앉다ㄵ → ㄴ[안따]to sit
여덟ㄼ → ㄹ[여덜]eight
닭ㄺ → ㄱ[닥]chicken
삶ㄻ → ㅁ[삼]life

🔗 Linking (연음, Yeon-eum)

When a syllable ending in a 받침 is followed by a syllable starting with ㅇ, the 받침 "links" forward and becomes the initial consonant of the next syllable. English does the same in fast speech — "an apple" slides into "a-napple".

WrittenLinkingPronouncedMeaning
먹어ㄱ links → 거머거 [meo-geo]eat (casual)
읽어ㄱ links → 거일거 [il-geo]read (casual)
한국어ㄱ links → 거한구거 [han-gu-geo]Korean language
음악ㅁ links → 막으막 [eu-mak]music
없어요ㅅ links (and tenses) → 써업써요 [eop-sseo-yo]there isn't

✅ Linking Rule

받침 + ㅇ = 받침 moves to become the initial consonant of the next syllable. This is the most common and most useful pronunciation rule. It makes Korean flow smoothly between syllables.

Two exceptions to the picture: a final ㅇ never moves (고양이 "cat" stays [고양이] — ㅇ is already a real "ng"), and a final ㅎ before a vowel simply disappears: 좋아요 → [조아요].

👃 Nasalization (비음화)

When certain consonants meet nasal consonants (ㄴ, ㅁ), they change to match. The stop consonants become their nasal counterparts — your nose is already open for the next sound, so it opens a moment early:

받침Before ㄴ or ㅁBecomesExamplePronounced
ㄱ [k]+ ㄴ/ㅁㅇ [ng]한국말한궁말 [han-gung-mal]
ㄷ [t]+ ㄴ/ㅁㄴ [n]받는반는 [ban-neun]
ㅂ [p]+ ㄴ/ㅁㅁ [m]합니다함니다 [ham-ni-da]

ㄹ joins in too: after ㅁ or ㅇ, a following ㄹ is said as ㄴ — 종로 (Jongno, a Seoul district) is [종노]. After ㄱ or ㅂ both sounds shift at once: 독립 (independence) → [동닙].

〰️ Liquidization (유음화)

When ㄴ and ㄹ meet in either order, the ㄴ becomes ㄹ and you hear a long, clear "ll":

WrittenPronouncedMeaning
설날[설랄] seol-lalLunar New Year
연락[열락] yeol-lakcontact, getting in touch
신라[실라] sil-laSilla (an ancient kingdom)

When ㅎ meets ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ or ㅈ — in either order — the two merge into one aspirated consonant. The ㅎ's breath gets "absorbed" into its neighbour:

CombinationResultExamplePronounced
ㄱ + ㅎㅋ축하추카 [chu-ka]
ㄷ (or ㅅ) + ㅎㅌ못해모태 [mo-tae]
ㅂ + ㅎㅍ입학이팍 [i-pak]
ㅈ + ㅎㅊ앉히다안치다 [an-chi-da]
ㅎ + ㄱㅋ좋고조코 [jo-ko]
ㅎ + ㄷㅌ좋다조타 [jo-ta]
ㅎ + ㅈㅊ좋지조치 [jo-chi]

💪 Tensification (경음화)

After a 받침 pronounced as [k], [t], or [p], the following plain consonant becomes tense (doubled):

WrittenChangePronouncedMeaning
학교ㄱ + ㄱ → ㄱ + ㄲ학꾜 [hak-kkyo]school
식당ㄱ + ㄷ → ㄱ + ㄸ식땅 [sik-ttang]restaurant
입구ㅂ + ㄱ → ㅂ + ㄲ입꾸 [ip-kku]entrance

Try saying 학교 slowly with a plain ㄱ in the middle: your throat is still tight from the closed [k], so the next consonant comes out tense whether you plan it or not. The rule describes what your mouth naturally does.

🔀 Palatalization (구개음화)

A final ㄷ or ㅌ followed by the vowel 이 turns into ㅈ or ㅊ. The "i" pulls the tongue up toward the palate, just as English "don't you" becomes "don-chu":

WrittenPronouncedMeaning
같이[가치] ga-chitogether
굳이[구지] gu-jistubbornly, necessarily
해돋이[해도지] hae-do-jisunrise

🇰🇷 Cultural Note — the official rulebook: These rules are not folk wisdom. South Korea's National Institute of Korean Language publishes the Standard Pronunciation rules (표준 발음법), and good Korean dictionaries print each word's pronunciation in square brackets, exactly like the columns on this page. Because spelling and sound part ways so often, even native speakers sometimes pause over how a word is spelled. If it feels like a lot, you're in good company.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Pronouncing every letter as written. 합니다 said "hap-ni-da" sounds robotic; natural Korean is [함니다].
  • Releasing final consonants. 밥 ends with closed lips, not "bap-uh". Hold the closure.
  • Spelling what you hear. The rules run from spelling to sound, not back. Write 국물, even though you say [궁물].
  • Linking a final ㅇ. 고양이 is go-yang-i, never "go-ya-ngi".
  • Forgetting the ㅎ. 좋다 is [조타], not "jo-da"; the ㅎ is silent but its breath moves to the ㄷ.

❓ Common Questions

Why doesn't Korean just spell words the way they sound?

Because the spelling shows meaning. 먹다 (to eat), 먹어요 (eats) and 먹는 (eating) are pronounced [먹따], [머거요] and [멍는], but all three are spelled with 먹, so you can instantly see they are the same verb. Phonetic spelling would hide that. Once you know the rules, the system gives you both the meaning and the sound.

Does romanization show these changes?

Mostly, yes. The Revised Romanization follows linking, nasalization, liquidization, palatalization and (in verbs and adjectives) aspiration: 국물 = gungmul, 신라 = Silla, 같이 = gachi. It deliberately ignores tensification, so 학교 stays hakgyo, not "hakkkyo".

Do I need to memorise the Korean names of the rules?

No. The names help when you look things up, but what matters is hearing and producing the changes. If you can say [궁물] and [함니다] naturally, you have learned the rule, whatever you call it.

Will people understand me if I get these wrong?

Usually, yes — context carries a lot. But these rules are the difference between sounding like you are reading and sounding like you are speaking. Practise the high-frequency words (감사합니다, 한국어, 맛있어요, 괜찮아요) until they come out automatically, and the rest will follow.
