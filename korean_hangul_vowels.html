🇰🇷 Lesson 2: Hangul Vowels & Syllable Blocks

In Lesson 1 you met the consonants and borrowed one vowel, ㅏ. Now you get the rest: all 21 vowels, and the one idea that makes Korean writing click — letters are stacked into syllable blocks, one block per syllable. By the end of this lesson you will be able to sound out almost any Korean word, even ones you don't know yet.

📖 How Vowels Work in Hangul

Korean vowels are built from three elements that King Sejong based on Neo-Confucian philosophy:

  • ㆍ (dot) — represents the sky/heaven (양, yang)
  • ㅡ (horizontal line) — represents the earth (음, yin)
  • ㅣ (vertical line) — represents a person standing

All vowels are combinations of these elements. Vowels are either vertical (written to the right of a consonant) or horizontal (written below a consonant).

The symbolism is not the whole story. The Hunminjeongeum Haerye also describes each base vowel by what the tongue does: for ㆍ the tongue is drawn back and the sound is deep; for ㅡ it is drawn back a little; for ㅣ it is not drawn back and the sound is shallow. So, like the consonants, the vowels combine philosophy with real phonetic observation. In modern print the dot has become a short stroke: ㅏ is ㅣ with the "heaven" stroke on the outside.

💡 Bright and dark vowels — a pattern you will use in Lesson 7

Where the short stroke sits matters. When it points outward or upward (ㅏ, ㅗ), the vowel counts as yang — Korean grammar books call these "bright" vowels. When it points inward or downward (ㅓ, ㅜ), the vowel is yin, or "dark". This is not trivia: Korean verb endings follow it. In Lesson 7 you'll see that verbs whose last vowel is ㅏ or ㅗ take one ending (-아요) and nearly everything else takes another (-어요). Remember the picture: the stroke on the sunny side means bright.

⚠️ ㅐ vs ㅔ (and ㅙ, ㅚ, ㅞ)

In modern Korean, ㅐ and ㅔ are pronounced nearly identically (both sound like "eh"). Young Koreans often can't distinguish them by sound alone — they rely on context and spelling. Don't stress about hearing the difference!

The same thing has happened to ㅙ, ㅚ and ㅞ: most Seoul speakers say all three as "weh". The official standard still describes ㅚ as a rounded single vowel, but you will rarely hear it. Learn the spelling of each word; your pronunciation will be fine.

💡 The three voices of ㅢ

ㅢ is the one vowel whose sound depends on where it stands:

  • First syllable, no consonant before it: the full "eu-i" — 의사 (uisa, doctor).
  • After a consonant: just "i" — 희망 is said [히망] (hope).
  • The possessive particle 의 ("of", "'s"): usually said like 에 ("e"). You'll meet it later in the course.

🧱 Building Syllable Blocks

Korean is written in syllable blocks. Each block is one syllable, combining a consonant + vowel (and optionally a final consonant). Every block must start with a consonant — if a syllable starts with a vowel sound, use ㅇ as a silent placeholder. Think of each block as a tiny box: the vowel's shape decides where everything goes.

💡 The ㅇ Placeholder

Since every block needs a consonant, vowel-initial syllables use ㅇ:

아 = ㅇ + ㅏ = "a"  ·  오 = ㅇ + ㅗ = "o"  ·  우 = ㅇ + ㅜ = "u"

✅ Writing Order

Write each block in reading order: initial consonant → vowel → final consonant, and inside each letter go top to bottom, left to right. Keep every block roughly square, whether it holds two letters (나) or four (닭, "chicken", with the double final ㄺ). Shrinking the letters to fit the box is what makes handwriting look Korean.

🧪 Try It: Build a Syllable

Hangul syllables aren't looked up in a table — they're computed. Unicode lays out all 11,172 possible blocks in one contiguous run, so any block's code point is arithmetic on its three parts. Pick an initial, a medial, and an optional final, and watch the block assemble.

The formula is 0xAC00 + (initial × 21 + medial) × 28 + final — 19 initials, 21 medials, 28 finals (index 0 meaning "no 받침").

🇰🇷 Cultural Note — the lost vowel: The "heaven" dot ㆍ (called 아래아, "lower a") was once a vowel in its own right. Its sound merged into others centuries ago, and the 1933 spelling standard dropped it from ordinary writing. It lives on in the vowel strokes you just learned, in some shop signs and brand logos that use it for an old-fashioned look, and in the Jeju language, where older speakers still preserve a distinct vowel for it.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • Writing a vowel on its own. ㅏ alone is a letter, not a syllable. "a" is written 아, with the silent ㅇ.
  • Mixing up ㅓ and ㅗ, or ㅜ and ㅡ. ㅓ (eo) is open, lips relaxed; ㅗ (o) has rounded lips. ㅜ (u) is rounded; ㅡ (eu) is spread, like a smile.
  • Putting the vowel on the wrong side. Vertical vowels (ㅏ ㅓ ㅣ …) go to the right; horizontal ones (ㅗ ㅜ ㅡ …) go underneath. 노 and 너 are different syllables.
  • Reading romanized "eo" as English "e-o". eo is a single sound, ㅓ. 어머니 is eo-meo-ni, three syllables, not five.
  • Adding a "w" or "y" glide to ㅗ. English "go" slides to "gow"; Korean 고 holds one pure vowel.

❓ Common Questions

If ㅐ and ㅔ sound the same, why does Korean keep both?

Because they used to be different, and spelling preserves the difference — just as English keeps "their" and "there". The spelling also carries meaning: 개 is "dog" and 게 is "crab". Learn each word with its spelling, and use context when listening.

Is "eo" (ㅓ) the same as the "o" in "hot"?

Close, but not quite. ㅓ is made with the mouth open and the lips unrounded, like the "u" in British "hut" but slightly further back. If your lips round, you are drifting toward ㅗ. A mirror helps: ㅓ relaxed, ㅗ rounded.

Do Koreans really use all 11,172 possible blocks?

No. The composer can build every combination, including oddities no word contains. Everyday Korean uses a much smaller set — the common South Korean character standard (KS X 1001) lists 2,350 precomposed syllables, which cover nearly all real text.

Should I rely on romanization?

Use it as training wheels this week, then let go. Romanization can't show many sound changes, and Korean speakers never use it among themselves. The sooner you read Hangul directly, the faster your reading and pronunciation improve.
