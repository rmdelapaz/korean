🇰🇷 Lesson 25: Describing: Modifiers & Comparisons

So far you can say The bag is big (가방이 커요). But you can't yet say a big bag, the movie I saw yesterday, the spiciest food, or Have you ever been to Jeju? This lesson unlocks all of them. It's one of the most rewarding lessons in the course: after it, your descriptions get richer, your questions get more interesting, and you'll start hearing these patterns everywhere in K-dramas and songs.

🧠 The Mental Model: Everything Goes Before the Noun

In English, short descriptions go before the noun ("a big bag") but longer ones go after it ("the movie that I saw yesterday"). Korean is beautifully consistent: all descriptions go before the noun. To make a verb or adjective describe a noun, you give its stem a modifier ending — -(으)ㄴ, -는 or -(으)ㄹ — and park it right in front of the noun.

💡 The Formula

[description + modifier ending] + NOUN

크다 + -ㄴ + 가방 → 큰 가방 (a big bag)

제가 어제 보다 + -ㄴ + 영화 → 제가 어제 본 영화 (the movie I saw yesterday)

Think of it as turning a whole sentence into one giant adjective. "I saw it yesterday" becomes a label stuck on the front of 영화.

🎨 Adjectives: -(으)ㄴ

Descriptive verbs (adjectives) take -ㄴ after a vowel and -은 after a consonant. Irregulars behave exactly as they did in Lesson 21, because -(으)ㄴ is a vowel-type ending.

⚠️ The 있다/없다 Exception

Adjectives built on 있다/없다 take -는, not -(으)ㄴ: 맛있다 → 맛있는 음식 (delicious food), 재미있다 → 재미있는 영화 (an interesting movie), 멋있다 → 멋있는 사람, 맛없다 → 맛없는 빵. The noun-verb 이다 becomes 인: 의사인 언니 ("my older sister, who is a doctor").

💡 Same Shape, Different Meaning

-(으)ㄴ means present on an adjective (큰 = big now) but past on a verb (먹은 = eaten). So you must know whether a word is a verb or an adjective. 좋다 (to be good) is an adjective → 좋은, but 좋아하다 (to like) is a verb → 좋아하는. And ㄹ-stems drop ㄹ before ㄴ and don't take 으: 살다 → 사는 (present), 산 (past), 살 (future); 알다 → 아는.

Now you know the secret behind two connectors from Lesson 23: 먹을 때 is literally "the time (I) will eat" and 먹은 후에 is "after (I) ate". Grammar you learned as set phrases just clicked into a system!

⚖️ Comparisons: 보다, 더, 제일, 만큼

Korean comparisons are simple: mark the thing you're comparing against with 보다 ("than"). 더 ("more") is optional when 보다 is there, but it's very common.

PatternKoreanEnglish
A이/가 B보다 (더) ~서울이 부산보다 더 커요.Seoul is bigger than Busan.
B보다 A이/가 ~ (order can flip)부산보다 서울이 커요.Seoul is bigger than Busan.
Verbs too저는 차보다 커피를 더 좋아해요.I like coffee more than tea.
덜 = less오늘은 어제보다 덜 추워요.Today is less cold than yesterday.
Which one…?커피하고 차 중에서 뭐가 더 좋아요?Between coffee and tea, which do you like more?
제일 / 가장 = most이 가방이 제일 비싸요.This bag is the most expensive.
Range + 에서 제일우리 반에서 민수 씨가 키가 제일 커요.Minsu is the tallest in our class.
만큼 = as … as동생도 저만큼 키가 커요.My younger sibling is as tall as me.
만큼 + negative부산은 서울만큼 크지 않아요.Busan isn't as big as Seoul.

제일 is the everyday word; 가장 means the same but sounds more written or formal. Combine them with modifiers for superlative descriptions: 제일 매운 음식 ("the spiciest food"), 가장 좋아하는 노래 ("my favorite song").

⏳ -고 있다: "Is Doing Right Now"

Attach -고 있다 to a verb stem to stress an action in progress, like English "-ing". Put the tense on 있다.

KoreanEnglish
지금 뭐 하고 있어요?What are you doing right now?
숙제하고 있어요.I'm doing homework.
요즘 한국어를 배우고 있어요.I'm learning Korean these days.
어제 전화했을 때 샤워하고 있었어요.When you called yesterday, I was showering.
할머니께서 주무시고 계세요.Grandma is sleeping. (honorific: -고 계시다)

Honest note: the plain present 공부해요 can already mean "I'm studying", so -고 있다 isn't required the way English "-ing" is — use it when you want to emphasize "in the middle of it". With wearing verbs it often describes a state: 코트를 입고 있어요 = "is wearing a coat".

🧪 -아/어 보다: "Try Doing"

Take the polite form, drop 요, and add 보다 ("to see"): "do it and see" = try it. With the past tense, it means "have (tried/been)".

KoreanEnglish
이 김치 한번 먹어 보세요.Try this kimchi.
이 옷 입어 봐도 돼요?May I try these clothes on?
이 노래 들어 보세요.Give this song a listen.
저는 제주도에 가 봤어요.I've been to Jeju Island.

🗺️ -(으)ㄴ 적이 있다/없다: "Have You Ever…?"

Past modifier + 적 ("occasion") + 이 있다/없다 = "there is/isn't an occasion when I did it" — that is, "I have / have never done it".

KoreanEnglish
한국에 간 적이 있어요?Have you ever been to Korea?
네, 두 번 간 적이 있어요.Yes, I've been twice.
김치를 만든 적이 없어요.I've never made kimchi.
이 노래를 들은 적이 있어요.I've heard this song before.
번데기를 먹어 본 적이 있어요?Have you ever tried beondegi (silkworm pupae)?

Combining -아/어 보다 with -(으)ㄴ 적이 있다 (먹어 본 적이 있어요) is extremely common — it stresses "tried it at least once". Use this pattern for real life experiences, not daily routines: saying 아침을 먹은 적이 있어요 ("I have eaten breakfast at some point in my life") sounds funny.

💬 Dialogue: Buying a Winter Coat

SpeakerKoreanEnglish
점원어서 오세요. 뭐 찾으세요?Welcome. What are you looking for?
손님겨울에 입을 코트를 찾고 있어요.I'm looking for a coat to wear in winter.
점원이 검은 코트는 어떠세요? 요즘 제일 인기 있는 코트예요.How about this black coat? It's the most popular coat these days.
손님예뻐요. 그런데 저 회색 코트보다 더 비싸요?It's pretty. But is it more expensive than that gray coat?
점원네, 조금 더 비싸요. 하지만 회색 코트보다 훨씬 따뜻해요.Yes, a bit more expensive. But it's much warmer than the gray one.
손님한번 입어 봐도 돼요?May I try it on?
점원그럼요. 거울은 저쪽에 있어요.Of course. The mirror is over there.
손님음, 저한테 좀 커요. 더 작은 사이즈 있어요?Hmm, it's a bit big on me. Do you have a smaller size?
점원네, 잠깐만요. 이거 입어 보세요.Yes, one moment. Try this one.
손님딱 좋아요! 이렇게 따뜻한 코트는 입어 본 적이 없어요. 이걸로 할게요.Perfect! I've never worn such a warm coat. I'll take this one.

Count the patterns: future modifier (입을 코트), -고 있다 (찾고 있어요), adjective modifiers (검은, 작은, 따뜻한), 있다 → 는 (인기 있는), comparisons (보다, 더, 제일, 훨씬 = "much"), -아 보다 and -(으)ㄴ 적이 없다. That's the whole lesson in one shopping trip.

🌏 Culture Note — "Have you ever tried…?" Expect to be asked 김치 먹어 본 적 있어요? or 매운 음식 잘 먹어요? within minutes of meeting Koreans. These aren't tests — they're friendly icebreakers and a way of sharing Korean culture with you. (In casual speech the particle 이 often drops: 먹어 본 적 있어요?) Answering 네, 먹어 봤어요! 정말 맛있었어요 will earn you a big smile — and probably an invitation to try something even spicier.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

✗ 재미있은 영화 → ✓ 재미있는 영화. (있다/없다 adjectives take -는.)

✗ 크는 가방 → ✓ 큰 가방. (Adjectives take -(으)ㄴ, not -는.)

✗ 덥은 날씨 → ✓ 더운 날씨. (ㅂ-irregular.)

✗ 만들은 케이크, 살는 곳 → ✓ 만든 케이크, 사는 곳. (ㄹ drops before ㄴ.)

✗ 영화 제가 어제 본 → ✓ 제가 어제 본 영화. (The description always comes first.)

✗ 부산이 서울보다 커요 (meaning "Seoul is bigger") → 보다 marks the one being out-done: ✓ 서울이 부산보다 커요.

Modifiers are where many learners feel their brain "flip" — you're building sentences backwards compared to English. It feels slow at first; that feeling is your brain rewiring, not a sign you're bad at this. Keep going.

❓ Common Questions

How do I know if a word is a verb or an adjective?

Go by meaning: if it describes a quality or state (big, cold, delicious, busy), it's an adjective and takes -(으)ㄴ; if it describes an action (eat, make, like, know), it's a verb and takes -는 in the present. Watch the tricky pairs where English uses one idea for both: 좋다 "to be good" (좋은) vs 좋아하다 "to like" (좋아하는); 예쁘다 "to be pretty" (예쁜) vs 예뻐하다 "to adore" (예뻐하는).

Can I stack several descriptions before one noun?

Yes. Join adjectives with -고: 싸고 맛있는 식당 ("a cheap and tasty restaurant"). You can also add a whole clause: 제가 자주 가는 싸고 맛있는 식당 ("the cheap, tasty restaurant I often go to"). Keep it to two layers at first so your listener can follow.

Do I always need 더 with 보다?

No. 서울이 부산보다 커요 is complete. 더 adds a little emphasis and is required when there's no 보다: 이게 더 좋아요 ("This one is better").

What's the difference between 가 봤어요 and 간 적이 있어요?

Both can mean "I've been there". 가 봤어요 is lighter and more conversational; 간 적이 있어요 explicitly frames it as a life experience ("there has been a time when…"). 가 본 적이 있어요 combines both and is very common.
