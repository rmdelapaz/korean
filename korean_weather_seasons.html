🇰🇷 Lesson 22: Weather & Seasons

Korea has four sharply different seasons: cherry blossoms in spring, a sticky monsoon summer, crisp autumn foliage and a dry, freezing winter. Koreans talk about the weather constantly, and it's the easiest small talk you'll ever have. There's also a grammar reason this lesson comes right after Lesson 21: the two most-used weather words, 덥다 (hot) and 춥다 (cold), are both ㅂ-irregular. Weather is where you'll practise that pattern until it's automatic.

🧩 Grammar in Action: ㅂ-Irregulars and Friends

From Lesson 21: when a stem ends in ㅂ and the ending starts with a vowel, many verbs turn that ㅂ into 우. Then 우 + 어요 contracts to 워요. Before a consonant, nothing changes.

Formula: ㅂ-irregular

덥 + 어요 → 더우 + 어요 → 더워요
춥 + 었어요 → 추우 + 었어요 → 추웠어요
덥 + ㄹ 거예요 → 더우 + ㄹ 거예요 → 더울 거예요
덥 + 지 않아요 → 덥지 않아요 (consonant ending: no change)

DictionaryPresentPast (L15)Future (L17)Negative (L10)
덥다 (hot)더워요더웠어요더울 거예요안 더워요 / 덥지 않아요
춥다 (cold)추워요추웠어요추울 거예요안 추워요 / 춥지 않아요
무덥다 (muggy)무더워요무더웠어요무더울 거예요안 무더워요

Why does this happen? Historically these stems ended in a soft sound between ㅂ and ㅜ. It survived as 우 before vowels and hardened into ㅂ before consonants. You don't need the history, just the rule: ㅂ + vowel → 우.

⚠️ Not every ㅂ-verb is irregular

입다 (to wear) is regular: 코트를 입어요, not 이워요. The same goes for 좁다 → 좁아요 and 잡다 → 잡아요. The weather adjectives 덥다, 춥다 and 무덥다 are all irregular, as are 가깝다 (가까워요) and 어렵다 (어려워요). When in doubt, check a dictionary: they mark irregulars.

💡 춥다 or 차갑다? 덥다 or 뜨겁다?

English "cold" and "hot" each do two jobs; Korean splits them. 춥다 / 덥다 describe the air, the weather, or how your body feels: 추워요! = "I'm cold!" 차갑다 / 뜨겁다 describe things you touch: 물이 차가워요 (the water is cold), 커피가 뜨거워요 (the coffee is hot). And yes, 차갑다 and 뜨겁다 are ㅂ-irregular too.

💡 장마 (Jangma): Monsoon Season

Korea's monsoon (장마) usually runs from late June to late July. Expect days of heavy rain, high humidity and occasional flooding. After it ends come the hottest weeks of the year, marked by the three 복날 days (초복, 중복, 말복). On those days people eat 삼계탕 (ginseng chicken soup), fighting heat with heat. There's even an idiom for it: 이열치열 (以熱治熱).

⚠️ 미세먼지 (Fine Dust)

Fine dust (미세먼지) is a real concern, especially in late winter and spring. Koreans check air-quality apps daily and wear masks on bad days. You'll hear 미세먼지가 심해요 (the fine dust is bad) and 오늘은 미세먼지가 나빠요 all the time. Check before planning outdoor activities!

💡 Culture Note: The first snow of the year (첫눈) is romantic in Korea. Couples promise to meet on the day it falls, and it's a favourite scene in K-dramas. In spring, weather forecasts include a cherry-blossom front (벚꽃 개화 시기) that tracks the blossoms moving north from Jeju to Seoul, and in autumn a foliage front does the same in reverse.

⚠️ Common Mistakes

  • 덥어요 / 춥어요 ❌ → 더워요 / 추워요 ✅. ㅂ becomes 우 before a vowel.
  • 더울을 거예요 or 덥을 거예요 ❌ → 더울 거예요 ✅. 더우 + ㄹ, with no extra 으.
  • 입워요 ❌ → 입어요 ✅. 입다 is a regular ㅂ-verb.
  • 커피가 더워요 ❌ → 커피가 뜨거워요 ✅. 덥다 is for weather, not for things you touch.
  • 비가 해요 / 비가 있어요 ❌ → 비가 와요 ✅. Rain and snow "come".
  • 불을 거예요 ❌ → 불 거예요 ✅. An ㄹ-stem already has its ㄹ.

Irregulars feel slippery at first. That's normal, not a sign you're "bad at grammar". Say 더워요, 추워요, 더울 거예요 aloud a dozen times today and they'll start to feel like single words.

❓ Common Questions

How do I say "I'm cold" — do I need 저는?

Just 추워요! is enough. Korean drops the obvious subject. 저는 추워요 is fine too if you're contrasting with someone else: 저는 추워요, 친구는 안 추워요.

Why is 맑아요 pronounced [말가요]?

맑 has a double 받침 ㄺ. Before a vowel, the second consonant (ㄱ) moves over to start the next syllable (Lesson 3's linking rule): 맑아요 → [말가요]. Before a consonant only ㄱ is heard: 맑다 → [막따].

What's the difference between 시원하다 and 춥다?

시원하다 is pleasantly cool, like a breeze on a hot day or a cold drink. Koreans even say 시원해요 about hot soup that feels refreshing! 춥다 is uncomfortably cold.

Can I use 눈 for both "snow" and "eye"?

Yes, they're homonyms, and context makes it clear: 눈이 와요 (it's snowing) vs 눈이 아파요 (my eyes hurt). In careful speech, snow 눈 is traditionally pronounced with a longer vowel, but most younger Seoul speakers no longer make that distinction.
